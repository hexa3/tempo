01 · The problem

Planners store your week.
None of them tell you if it fits.

We underestimate.

People reliably predict tasks will take less time than they do. Psychologists call it the planning fallacy (Buehler, Griffin & Ross, 1994).

Tempo learns your personal time multiplier per subject from the sessions you log.

We cram.

Spreading study over several days beats one long session the night before (Cepeda et al., 2006), but cramming is what usually happens.

Tempo splits exam prep across days: overview, active recall, then a practice test.

We find out too late.

An impossible week looks fine on Monday. You only discover it on Wednesday night, when there are no good options left.

Tempo spots it on day one and offers options it has already checked: more time, an extension, or a focused version.

03 · The AI

Three jobs, and the right
kind of AI for each.

Language model

Understanding what you type

Pick the engine at the top of the planner: Local AI (Qwen 2.5 3B through Ollama, so the text never leaves the laptop) or Groq (gpt-oss-120b in the cloud). Both reply in a fixed JSON schema.

Small models are bad at date arithmetic, so the model has to quote the student's words. Tempo's tested rules then work out the dates and check every quote against what was actually typed.

Statistical model

Predicting how long things take you

m = expΣ ln ri + k · ln m0n + k

r = actual ÷ planned minutes for each logged session · m0 = your overall habit · k = 3

With no data Tempo uses typical times. Each logged session pulls the estimate toward reality, and the range narrows as it learns.

Planning algorithm

Building a plan that never breaks the rules

Earliest-deadline-first with a slack test, spaced exam prep, a safety buffer before deadlines, subject switching, and a sleep window it never crosses.

When a week can't fit, it tries each option and only offers the ones that really work.

Why not let a chatbot write the schedule?

A plan must never double-book you, run past your bedtime, or quietly drop work. A language model can't promise that. An algorithm can, and our tests check it on every plan. So the AI reads and explains, and the algorithm schedules.

04 · Testing & improvement

We tested it, and it broke.
Here's one of those times.

We typed chem test next friday, english essay due monday about 2 hours, robotics tue and thu 4-5:30 into the local model on Thursday 5 November.

What the 3B model returned

  • Chemistry test: Wed 18 Nov "next Friday" isn't a Wednesday
  • English essay: Tue 10 Nov Monday is the 9th
  • Chemistry test: about 2 hours that was the essay's estimate
  • Task: "Robotics practice" it's an activity, not homework
  • "I only have 2 hours on weekdays" the student never said this

What the student sees after Tempo's checks

  • Chemistry test: Fri 13 Nov date corrected, flagged for confirmation
  • English essay: Mon 9 Nov, 2h
  • Chemistry test: no estimate Tempo will predict one
  • Robotics: Tue & Thu 4:00–5:30pm, once, as an activity
  • Study time: unchanged

The fix: every phrase the AI claims to quote must actually appear in what the student typed, and code we can test does the date maths. This exact model reply is now an automated test. Read all eight stories →

05 · Responsible design

Built for students,
not for engagement.

Private by default

A nickname, not a name. Everything is stored in the browser, the server keeps no logs of what students type, and there's a switch that never sends text online.

It won't do your homework

"Write my essay" gets a refusal and an offer to plan it instead. The same rule runs before every AI engine.

Kind when it's hard

A low-energy check-in lightens the day, and crunch weeks are flagged. Stress gets a kind reply that points to a counsellor or a trusted adult.

Honest about limits

Every estimate shows a range and a confidence level. Every session explains why it's there. A week that doesn't fit is never hidden.

You stay in charge

The student confirms every task the AI extracts, and picks the trade-off when a week is too full.

Accessible

Works with just a keyboard, plus large text, high contrast, extra spacing for dyslexia, dark mode, reduced motion, voice input and read-aloud.

Try it on the judges' scenario.

Three assignments, two exams, two activities and two hours a day are already loaded. Then change something and watch it re-plan.
